# Quality Benchmarks

The difference between generating work and generating work the owner signs.
Each section: what great is, the tells of average, and checkable tests.
Scorecard dimension 6 scores against the relevant section. Sections grow
only from real work (a benchmark never used in a critique within a year is
pruned to archive).

## What makes a premium brand

Great: one idea repeated with discipline across every touchpoint for years —
palette ≤ 3 colors, ≤ 2 typefaces, one photographic voice; a point of view
that visibly excludes someone; generosity where the customer feels it
(materials, response time, packaging, silence) and restraint everywhere
else; prices stated with confidence, never apologized for.
**Average tells:** trend-templated look; "quality you can trust" claims that
name no specifics; voice that changes per channel; crowded layouts; stock-feel
imagery; discount-led communication.
**Tests:** cover the logo — still recognizable from tone and layout alone?
Read 5 touchpoints side by side — same brand, same person speaking? Would
raising prices 20% feel congruent with how it presents?

## What makes an average brand (kept explicit as the anti-benchmark)

Average is not broken — it is **interchangeable**. Correct grammar, current
trends, pleasant palette, claims every competitor also makes. The test of
average: swap the logo for a competitor's and nothing else needs to change.
Atlas ships nothing that passes the logo-swap test.

## What makes a landing page convert

Great: one page, one job, one action — the CTA is the only decision offered;
headline states the specific outcome in the visitor's own words (personas'
"language they use"); proof sits adjacent to the claim it proves (specific:
numbers, names, artifacts — never a wall of logos alone); objections answered
in the order the persona raises them; friction inventory minimized (every
form field re-justified); fast (< 2s meaningful paint) and designed
mobile-first.
**Average tells:** headline names the product instead of the outcome;
multiple competing CTAs; testimonials that praise without specifics;
"learn more".
**Tests:** 5-second test — a cold persona can say what it is, who it's for,
what to do next. Count decisions offered: more than one = not a landing
page. Every claim has proof within one viewport of it.

## What makes a logo timeless

Great: works in a single color first — color is costume, form is identity;
legible at 16 px and at 10 meters; distinct silhouette (recognizable
blurred); appropriate to the business without illustrating it literally; no
technique-of-the-year (gradients, bevels, currently-fashionable geometry);
sits comfortably beside marks that are 50 years old.
**Average tells:** needs its color to be recognized; describes the product
literally; crowded detail that dies at small sizes; looks like this year's
Dribbble.
**Tests:** photocopy test (1-color, small — survives?); blur test
(silhouette holds?); date test — would it embarrass in a 1995 annual report
or a 2035 app icon?

## What makes typography feel luxurious

Great: space is the signal — generous margins, airy leading, narrow measure
(45-75 characters); few sizes (a luxury page rarely needs more than three);
true cuts, not fakes — real italics, real small caps, letterspaced capitals;
one voice family carried with total consistency; restraint in weight
contrast — luxury whispers, it does not bold.
**Average tells:** faux bold/italic; five sizes on one screen; tight margins
that fill every pixel; novelty display faces; ALL CAPS without
letterspacing.
**Tests:** remove every image — does the text page alone feel expensive?
Count type sizes on one screen: > 4 = functional at best, noisy at worst.

## What makes copy persuasive without sounding salesy

Great: specificity does the selling — numbers, names, scenarios in the
reader's vocabulary; claim and evidence travel together in the same breath;
verbs over adjectives ("cuts invoicing to 10 minutes" not "incredibly
efficient"); one idea per sentence; states honestly who it is *not* for —
exclusion is the most credible claim available; the CTA is the reader's
obvious next step, not pressure ("see the pricing" not "don't miss out").
**Average tells:** superlatives without evidence; manufactured urgency
(anti-pattern); addressing "businesses like yours"; benefits lists that fit
any product; exclamation points doing the work evidence should.
**Tests:** strike every adjective — does the argument still stand? Read
aloud to the persona — cringe anywhere is a cut. Could a competitor paste
this under their logo? Then it says nothing.

## What makes a presentation world-class

Great: an argument, not a stack — stakes stated inside the first two
minutes, tension resolved by the end, one decision requested explicitly;
slide = billboard (one idea, ≤ 20 visible words, detail lives in notes or a
leave-behind); every chart makes exactly one point, stated in its title,
labeled directly (no legend-hunting); consistent grid and brand palette so
design disappears; ends on the ask, not on "thank you".
**Average tells:** agenda slide as the opener; paragraphs on slides read
aloud; charts that require study; clip-art energy; no explicit ask.
**Tests:** flip deck in 30 seconds reading only titles — do the titles alone
make the argument? Is the requested decision writable in one sentence? Any
slide the presenter reads verbatim = a document, cut or move it.

## What never ships (to a client or to production)

Absolute — these are the QUALITY_STANDARD hard blockers plus their
worked-out cases:

1. Anything with an unresolved [VERIFY] or an invented fact.
2. Unlicensed or unmanifested assets — "found online" is exposure, not sourcing.
3. First drafts. No artifact skips the critique cycle, ever, deadline or not.
4. Work failing the logo-swap test — interchangeable output at premium price.
5. Anything on taste/anti-patterns.md — AI-flavored prose, borrowed
   authority, fake urgency, unearned "we", feature-first selling.
6. Work the owner would not sign publicly at full price with competitors
   watching (DESIGN_PHILOSOPHY.md bar).
7. Broken details in the first minute of inspection — typos, dead links,
   misalignment. If the visible craft is careless, the invisible work is
   presumed careless too.
8. Anything that mixes luxury and functional languages unintentionally —
   language is chosen by the business DNA, checked at critique.
