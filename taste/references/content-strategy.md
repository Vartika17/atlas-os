# Content Strategy — Reference Study

Content strategy treats publishing as an owned asset: recurring editorial value that compounds
trust and lowers acquisition cost the longer it runs. The commercial test is durability — the
exemplars below are programs still paying out decades after launch, across every media shift.

## The Furrow (John Deere, 1895–present)

- **Why world-class:** A farm journal published continuously since 1895, reported at millions of
  circulation at peak and still published in dozens of countries. It survived print, radio, TV,
  and the web because it was never a catalog — it is the standard ancestor case for the entire
  discipline.
- **Design principles:** Agronomy journalism first, equipment a distant second; edited and staffed
  like a real magazine; stories about the farmer's business and land, with product mentions earned
  by relevance rather than inserted by quota.
- **Business problem solved:** Tractors are high-ticket, trust-dependent, and bought perhaps once
  a decade. The Furrow keeps Deere useful — and present — during the long years between buying
  moments.
- **Psychology:** Reciprocity accumulated over decades; authority through genuinely applicable
  expertise; positive-valence mere exposure — the brand is associated with the reader's own
  profitability, not with being sold to.
- **UX principles:** Magazine pacing, feature structure, real photography — designed to be kept on
  the kitchen table, not skimmed and discarded.
- **Branding principles:** Deere positioned as ally of the farming business, not vendor of green
  machines; the restraint on product pushing is itself the positioning.
- **Visual principles:** High production standards signal respect for the reader and rub off on
  perceived machine quality.
- **Learn:** Serve the reader's profit-and-loss, not your catalog, and match publishing cadence to
  the trust cycle rather than the sales cycle.
- **Do NOT copy:** Print-era economics and 1895's near-zero competition — "a helpful blog" is now
  table stakes, and differentiation requires editorial quality or proprietary data, not mere
  helpfulness.
- **Suitable industries:** High-ticket infrequent purchases — equipment, real estate, enterprise
  software, healthcare, financial planning.

## Michelin Guide (Michelin, 1900–present)

- **Why world-class:** A tire company's free motoring guide, launched when France reportedly had
  only a few thousand cars, became the world's reference authority on restaurants — 120+ years of
  continuous operation, with the stars now conferring more prestige than most food media. The
  logic: more reasons to drive, more kilometers, more tire wear.
- **Design principles:** Content aimed one step up the demand chain (travel causes driving causes
  tire purchases); a rating system with scarce, defensible standards enforced by anonymous
  inspectors; practical utility bundled in — maps, mechanics, hotels — so the object earns
  permanent glovebox space.
- **Business problem solved:** Category demand creation. When the constraint on tire sales was the
  number of reasons to drive, Michelin manufactured reasons — "worth a special journey" is
  literally an instruction to burn rubber.
- **Psychology:** Scarcity of stars; authority through opaque, rigorous, incorruptible judgment;
  goal-gradient — a destination rating converts an idle weekend into a drive.
- **UX principles:** Reference-book structure with standardized symbols legible at a glance; the
  star system is an information-design asset that compresses judgment into one glyph.
- **Branding principles:** Rigor halo — the guide's exactingness transfers to the tires; the brand
  funds content that never mentions the product, which is exactly why it is believed.
- **Visual principles:** Fixed iconography and portable format, unchanged long enough to become
  the category's visual language.
- **Learn:** If your product is a derivative of an activity, grow the activity. A standard or
  rating system is content with a moat — competitors cannot copy your accumulated history of
  judgment.
- **Do NOT copy:** Century-scale patience and anonymous-inspector economics; loss-leader content
  at this scale needs an explicit demand-chain thesis, not faith that "brand awareness" will pay.
- **Suitable industries:** Enabling and infrastructure products — payments, cloud, developer
  tools, travel, logistics — anything that monetizes an activity it can stimulate.

## They Ask, You Answer (Marcus Sheridan, River Pools & Spas, 2009–present)

- **Why world-class:** A Virginia fiberglass-pool installer near failure in the 2008 crisis
  committed to answering every real buyer question in plain text — including price and the
  product's problems. Sheridan reported that a single article answering the cost question drove
  millions in tracked sales; the approach became a documented framework (book, 2017) replicated by
  practitioners for over a decade.
- **Design principles:** The "Big 5" topics — cost, problems, comparisons, reviews, best-of;
  candor about the product's genuine downsides; content mapped one-to-one to sales-call
  questions; assignment selling — prospects read specific pages before the sales call.
- **Business problem solved:** A local high-ticket service with anxious buyers and total
  information asymmetry; the content shortens the sales cycle by arriving at the call with
  objections already answered.
- **Psychology:** Two-sided argument effect — admitting flaws raises credibility of every other
  claim; answering the price question defuses the buyer's largest anxiety; the honest vendor wins
  by default in a category trained to hide.
- **UX principles:** The question is the headline, verbatim as buyers phrase it; the answer comes
  first, detail after; plain language over trade jargon.
- **Branding principles:** Transparency as positioning — the company that answers what
  competitors are afraid to publish.
- **Visual principles:** n/a
- **Learn:** Your sales team's inbox is the content calendar. The article answering the scariest
  question earns trust no volume of safe content can buy.
- **Do NOT copy:** 2009-era SEO ease — cost-question rankings are now contested and partially
  absorbed by AI answers; text alone underperforms where buyers expect video and calculators;
  honesty as a tactic without operational transparency behind it gets found out.
- **Suitable industries:** Considered local services, home improvement, healthcare, legal, B2B
  with opaque pricing, SaaS.

## Timeless principles extracted

- Publish for the reader's outcomes, not your catalog: product-free usefulness compounds;
  catalog-shaped content depreciates the day it ships.
- Answer the questions buyers actually ask, starting with the ones competitors are afraid of —
  price, problems, and honest comparisons.
- Match cadence to the trust cycle, not the sales cycle: the higher the ticket and the rarer the
  purchase, the longer the presence must run before it pays.
- Build an authority asset — a standard, rating, dataset, or benchmark — because accumulated
  judgment is the one content format competitors cannot fast-follow.
- When category demand is the constraint, grow the activity your product monetizes, one step up
  the demand chain, rather than shouting louder at existing demand.
- Apply the ten-year test: a program you cannot imagine funding for a decade is a campaign, not a
  content strategy, and should be budgeted as one.
