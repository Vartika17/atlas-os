# Presentation Design — Reference Study

A presentation is a decision-manufacturing device: its commercial value is what the room decides,
remembers, and repeats afterward. The exemplars below are methods proven at the highest stakes —
category-defining launches and trillion-dollar-company decision meetings — not slide aesthetics.

## Steve Jobs keynote method (Apple, 1998–2011)

- **Why world-class:** Apple launches (iPod 2001, iPhone 2007) functioned as earned-media events
  reported to be worth hundreds of millions in equivalent coverage; the 2007 iPhone keynote
  remains the most-studied product launch on record, and the method is documented in detail
  (Gallo, 2009).
- **Design principles:** One idea per slide — often one word, number, or image; the headline
  written first as a repeatable sentence ("1,000 songs in your pocket"); rule of three for
  agenda structure; villain-then-hero arc (the problem demonstrated, the product as resolution);
  live demo as centerpiece; "one more thing" as an engineered peak-end.
- **Business problem solved:** Compressing complex products into sentences that journalists and
  customers would retell verbatim — the presentation itself was the distribution strategy.
- **Psychology:** Picture-superiority effect; numbers translated to human scale (songs, not
  gigabytes); contrast structure sustains attention; peak-end rule shapes what is remembered;
  extreme rehearsal reads as ease and lowers the audience's processing effort.
- **UX principles:** Near-zero text means the speaker, not the deck, carries the information —
  the audience is never forced to read and listen at once, which is the core failure of most
  business slides.
- **Branding principles:** Deck aesthetics identical to product aesthetics — sparse, black,
  precise; the presentation is the product's first demo of its own values.
- **Visual principles:** Black background, one centered element, very large type, generous
  emptiness; slides as backdrop to a performance, never as a leave-behind document.
- **Learn:** Write the press headline first and build the talk to earn it; a working demo beats
  any description of the demo.
- **Do NOT copy:** Messiah staging and "revolutionary" inflation invite parody without Apple's
  product surprise to back them; reported weeks of rehearsal do not fit a weekly business review;
  hiding all supporting data works only when the speaker's authority is beyond question.
- **Suitable industries:** Product launches, fundraising pitches, conference keynotes, sales
  kickoffs — any one-to-many persuasion moment.

## Duarte sparkline (Nancy Duarte, Resonate, 2010)

- **Why world-class:** A framework reverse-engineered from speeches with proven decades-long
  durability — King's "I Have a Dream," Jobs's 2007 keynote — revealing a shared shape: repeated
  alternation between "what is" and "what could be," resolving in a "new bliss." Duarte's firm
  applied the method commercially at scale (including the slide work behind An Inconvenient
  Truth), and it has been standard in corporate communications for 15 years.
- **Design principles:** Structure as oscillation — current state versus better future, opened
  and closed repeatedly to create tension-release cycles; the audience cast as hero, the
  presenter as mentor; one engineered "S.T.A.R. moment" (something they'll always remember) per
  talk; a call to adventure at the start and a picture of the new world at the end.
- **Business problem solved:** Moving resistant audiences to act — change programs, sales,
  investment — where accurate information alone demonstrably moves no one.
- **Psychology:** Tension-resolution loops sustain attention across long talks; contrast makes
  the future state feel urgent rather than optional; mentor-positioning defuses the status
  resistance that lecturing provokes.
- **UX principles:** The talk is designed on an audience-energy timeline before any slide exists;
  pacing is a first-class design object, not a byproduct.
- **Branding principles:** n/a
- **Visual principles:** Message architecture precedes visuals; slides inherit one-idea-per-slide
  discipline from the same analysis of what audiences can absorb.
- **Learn:** Structure any persuasive talk as repeated gap-open/gap-close between today and the
  promised state, and deliberately design the single moment people will retell.
- **Do NOT copy:** Full hero's-journey staging on a ten-minute status update; manufactured
  S.T.A.R. moments — props, stunts, planted emotion — read as fake the moment substance runs
  thin.
- **Suitable industries:** Change initiatives, enterprise sales, fundraising, advocacy and
  nonprofit work, internal transformation programs.

## Amazon narrative six-pager (Amazon S-team, 2004–present)

- **Why world-class:** Bezos banned slide decks for senior-team meetings in 2004 (documented in
  shareholder letters and Working Backwards); the six-page narrative memo persisted for two
  decades while the company scaled a thousandfold, and its PR/FAQ variant sits under product
  bets including AWS and Kindle. Twenty years of daily institutional use is stronger evidence
  than any single famous deck.
- **Design principles:** Full sentences and paragraphs force causal reasoning that bullets let
  authors skip; a hard six-page limit forces prioritization; twenty minutes of silent reading
  opens the meeting so everyone debates from the same information; questions after reading,
  decision in the same room; data lives in appendices.
- **Business problem solved:** High-stakes decisions distorted by presenter charisma and
  bullet-point vagueness, and meeting hours consumed by context transfer instead of judgment.
- **Psychology:** Writing exposes missing logic that outlines conceal; silent reading strips the
  status effects of live performance; narrative form forces the author to pre-experience the
  audience's objections and answer them in advance.
- **UX principles:** The memo is the interface — self-contained, skimmable headers, one argument
  per section, evidence retrievable but not obstructing the argument.
- **Branding principles:** n/a
- **Visual principles:** Deliberately none — the absence of visuals is the design decision; it
  removes production polish as a variable, so ideas compete on reasoning alone.
- **Learn:** Match format to job — persuasion theater for launches, prose for decisions. An idea
  that cannot survive being written in full sentences is not ready to be decided on.
- **Do NOT copy:** Six-pagers for audiences who will not commit twenty minutes of reading;
  imposing memo culture without executive enforcement produces documents nobody reads; the
  format is wrong for inspiration, launches, and large audiences.
- **Suitable industries:** Board and executive decisions, product strategy, investment memos,
  engineering design reviews — any small-room, high-stakes decision.

## Timeless principles extracted

- Decide the artifact's job before its form: decks persuade rooms, documents make decisions;
  using either for the other's job fails both audiences.
- One idea per unit — slide, section, or paragraph. An audience can read or listen, never both;
  every design choice flows from that constraint.
- Write the sentence the audience should repeat afterward before building anything, then make
  every element earn that sentence or cut it.
- Structure is tension management: alternate current pain with possible future in cycles; flat
  sequences of information lose any room regardless of visual polish.
- Show over tell at the climax — a live demo, a worked example, a moment engineered to be
  retold; what gets repeated afterward is the presentation's actual output.
- Production gloss cannot rescue missing logic: rehearse and rewrite the thinking, because the
  audience forgives plain slides but never forgives an argument that does not hold.
