# Product Design — Reference Study

Excellence in physical product design means the object explains itself, earns trust before it is touched, and stays useful long after its styling era ends. A business portfolio should care because durable design compounds: it cuts support cost, extends replacement cycles into loyalty, and lets one design language carry hundreds of SKUs. The exemplars below all left written methodologies, not just famous objects.

## Braun design language (Dieter Rams and design team, 1955–1995)

- **Why world-class:** One coherent system governed radios, shavers, calculators, and clocks for four decades. Rams's team designed the *rules* first — grids, radii, control hierarchy, color discipline (grey/white/black plus one signal color) — so every product read as a Braun before the logo was found. The SK4 phonograph (1956) and ET66 calculator (1987) still look contemporary.
- **Design principles:** Rams's Ten Principles of Good Design (formalized late 1970s):
  - Good design is innovative
  - Good design makes a product useful
  - Good design is aesthetic
  - Good design makes a product understandable
  - Good design is unobtrusive
  - Good design is honest
  - Good design is long-lasting
  - Good design is thorough down to the last detail
  - Good design is environmentally friendly
  - Good design is as little design as possible
- **Business problem solved:** Postwar consumer electronics were a commodity sold on ornament. Braun differentiated on clarity and trust, commanded premium prices with lower styling churn, and amortized one design system across the whole catalog.
- **Psychology:** Restraint signals competence — the product that does not shout implies it does not need to. Predictable control layouts build habit; honesty (no fake materials, no implied features) prevents the trust collapse that follows discovery of a lie.
- **UX principles:** One primary control per task; hierarchy by size and position, not decoration; states visible at a glance (the SK4's transparent lid made operation literally visible). Understandability treated as a feature, not documentation's job.
- **Branding principles:** The product *is* the brand asset. System consistency across categories replaced advertising-led identity; the logo could be small because the design language was loud.
- **Visual principles:** Strict grid alignment; controls sized proportionally to importance; near-monochrome fields so a single accent color carries all signaling; radii and typography identical across the range.
- **Learn:** Write the rules of your design language down and enforce them across the portfolio; the system outlives any single product and any single designer.
- **Do NOT copy:** The specific 1960s minimal aesthetic as a costume. Grey boxes and Akzidenz-Grotesk do not confer Braun's discipline — the discipline was the deleted features, not the look.
- **Suitable industries:** Consumer electronics, appliances, medical devices, SaaS product suites, industrial equipment — anywhere a portfolio must read as one family.

## Eames Office furniture program (Charles & Ray Eames / Herman Miller, 1945–1978)

- **Why world-class:** Turned wartime materials research (molded plywood leg splints) into mass-produced furniture that is still in continuous production 70+ years later. The molded plywood chairs, fiberglass shells, and the Lounge Chair each solved manufacturing problems, not styling briefs.
- **Design principles:** Documented in the Eameses' own writing and films:
  - "The best, for the most, for the least" — quality at mass scale as the explicit goal
  - Constraints embraced as the design brief ("design depends largely on constraints")
  - Iterative prototyping — years of test rigs (the "Kazam! machine") before release
  - The connection as the design problem: joints, shock mounts, and bases engineered first
  - "Take your pleasure seriously" — rigor and play treated as the same activity
- **Business problem solved:** Herman Miller needed post-war product lines that machines, not craftsmen, could build. The Eameses made low-cost mass production a quality story instead of a compromise story, giving one manufacturer decades of evergreen SKUs.
- **Psychology:** Molded forms fit the body before the eye — comfort discovered by sitting converts skeptics. Visible structure (exposed shock mounts, wire bases) reads as candor; the object seems to have nothing to hide.
- **UX principles:** Ergonomics derived from body casts and prototype testing, not styling; chairs ganged, stacked, and shipped flat — the "use lifecycle" (delivery, storage, cleaning) designed alongside the sitting.
- **Branding principles:** Designer-as-brand handled honestly: the Eames name certified a process (research, film, exhibition, product all one practice), not a signature flourish.
- **Visual principles:** Thin structure under generous volume — visual lightness from separating shell and base; material left frank (plywood edges exposed, fiberglass fibers visible); color as a cataloged system of options, not one-off decoration.
- **Learn:** Put your R&D into the joint — the hardest engineering problem — and the form will follow; longevity comes from solving manufacture and use, not from styling.
- **Do NOT copy:** Mid-century silhouettes as nostalgia. An Eames-shaped chair without the materials research is borrowed authority; the original forms were outputs of process, not a mood board.
- **Suitable industries:** Furniture, hardware, automotive interiors, construction systems, any product where unit economics at scale and durability must coexist.

## OXO Good Grips (Sam Farber with Smart Design, 1990–present)

- **Why world-class:** A vegetable peeler designed for a founder's arthritic wife outsold every "normal" peeler because the accommodation helped everyone. Grew into 500+ SKUs governed by one inclusive-design method, and pushed Universal Design from rehabilitation literature into mainstream retail.
- **Design principles:** Aligned with the Center for Universal Design's seven principles (1997), applied commercially:
  - Equitable use — one product for all hands, no stigmatizing "special needs" variant
  - Tolerance for error and low physical effort — large diameter, low grip force
  - Perceptible information — the fins and oval section signal "grip here, this way"
  - Test with the extremes (arthritis, low vision) to improve the mean
  - Simple and intuitive use — operation obvious regardless of experience or literacy
- **Business problem solved:** Kitchen tools were a race-to-the-bottom commodity. Designing for the hardest-to-serve user created a defensible premium tier in a category with no brand loyalty — reported as one of housewares' fastest-growing brands through the 1990s.
- **Psychology:** The oversized santoprene handle *looks* comfortable before it is held — affordance as shelf-level advertising. Buying it feels like self-care, not disability accommodation, because the framing is universal.
- **UX principles:** Grip diameter, flexible fins, and oval cross-section derived from hand-limitation research; the tool orients itself in the hand without looking. Error tolerance designed in (blade guards, non-slip everything).
- **Branding principles:** The handle is the trademark — a functional feature doing the brand's identification work across hundreds of products, more consistent than any logo program.
- **Visual principles:** Black elastomer signals the grip zone by contrast; proportions deliberately generous where competitors economized, making the value visible; form vocabulary repeated verbatim across the line.
- **Learn:** Design for the edge-case user and the mainstream inherits the benefit — inclusive design is a market-expansion strategy, not a compliance cost.
- **Do NOT copy:** Rubberized black handles as a finish. The equity was the research into limited hands; the santoprene is just where that research surfaced.
- **Suitable industries:** Housewares, medical and assistive devices, tools, packaging, fintech onboarding, any interface where ability varies widely across the customer base.

## Timeless principles extracted

- Design the system before the product: written rules (grid, hierarchy, color, radii) that govern a whole portfolio outlast any single hit and make every future SKU cheaper to design.
- Honesty is a durability strategy: never imply materials, features, or performance the product lacks — discovered lies destroy more value than the lie added, and honest products age without embarrassment.
- Solve the hardest constraint first (the joint, the manufacture, the weakest hand); form derived from a solved constraint stays valid as long as the constraint does, while styled form expires with its trend.
- Make state and operation visible: a product that explains itself at a glance converts at the shelf, cuts support cost, and builds the habitual trust that repeat purchase depends on.
- Design for the extreme user to win the average one — accommodation framed universally expands the market; accommodation framed as "special" shrinks it to a niche.
- Restraint signals competence: every element you delete increases the perceived confidence of what remains, so justify each feature, control, and graphic or remove it.
