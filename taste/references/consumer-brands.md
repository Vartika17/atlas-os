# Consumer Brands — Reference Study

Consumer brand excellence is mass-market memory engineering: being mentally available (thought
of first) and physically available (easy to buy) to the largest possible population, for
decades. The winners are not the most loved brands but the most easily remembered and least
often ruled out. For a portfolio, this category teaches the economics of distinctiveness —
assets that make advertising cheaper and shelf presence self-reinforcing.

## Coca-Cola (script by Frank Mason Robinson, 1886; red-and-script system stable since the early 1900s)

- **Why world-class:** The Spencerian script wordmark has been in continuous use since the 1880s — through two
  world wars, quarterly reformulation pressure, and every design fashion — making it one of the oldest
  unchanged commercial marks in existence. The 1985 New Coke reversal demonstrated, at enormous reported cost,
  that the brand's value sat in memory and ritual, not the liquid; the company has protected the memory
  structure ever since.
- **Design principles:** Never redesign what is already remembered; single-color ownership executed at total
  scale (red at the fridge, the truck, the stadium); the identity system tolerates infinite local translation
  because script + red + ribbon device carry recognition even in non-Latin alphabets.
- **Business problem solved:** A near-commodity flavored syrup needed a defensible moat; the moat became
  universal recognition plus ubiquitous availability, letting Coca-Cola price above private-label copies that
  taste similar in blind tests.
- **Psychology:** Mere exposure and processing fluency at civilization scale; conditioned association with
  occasions (meals, holidays — the brand's role in codifying the red-suited Santa in 1930s advertising is a
  documented campaign, not a myth about inventing him); loss aversion revealed by New Coke — people defended a
  memory, not a taste.
- **UX principles:** n/a
- **Branding principles:** Distinctive assets over messages — campaigns change, assets never; advertise to
  everyone, not a segment; refresh executions constantly while the memory structure stays frozen.
- **Visual principles:** Handwritten script as an ownable shape no typeface can reproduce; a red that
  functions as packaging, media, and architecture; the dynamic ribbon as a secondary device when the script is
  absent.
- **Learn:** For mass brands, memory is the product. Every asset decision should be scored on one axis: does
  it strengthen or spend existing memory structure?
- **Do NOT copy:** The strategy of meaning-free ubiquity — it requires a century of media spend and total
  distribution. A challenger copying "just be everywhere, say nothing" has neither the reach nor the memory to
  make it work.
- **Suitable industries:** FMCG, beverages, snacks, household staples — high-frequency, low-consideration
  purchases where availability and recall decide.

## Nike (swoosh by Carolyn Davidson, 1971; "Just Do It" by Wieden+Kennedy, 1988)

- **Why world-class:** A $35 student-designed checkmark became a mark so recognized the company routinely
  drops its own name — logo-only branding on products and ads since the mid-1990s. "Just Do It" has run for
  over 35 years as a platform elastic enough to hold championship athletes and first-time joggers in the same
  sentence. The brand sells effort, not shoes; products rotate, the idea does not.
- **Design principles:** Radical simplicity of mark (one stroke, direction, motion) so it survives embroidery,
  molding, and single-pixel rendering; verbal identity as infrastructure — the tagline is a reusable platform,
  not a campaign line; athletes as the visual system's real content.
- **Business problem solved:** Athletic shoes are a technical arms race with fast-copying competitors; Nike
  moved the purchase decision from product specs to self-image ("the brand for people who do"), making
  comparison shopping partly irrelevant and sustaining price premiums across product generations.
- **Psychology:** Aspirational identity and self-signaling — wearing the swoosh is a statement about intent,
  not achievement, which makes the market everyone; emotional arousal in advertising increases memorability;
  the mark's motion shape carries meaning (speed) pre-verbally.
- **UX principles:** n/a
- **Branding principles:** Say one thing for decades through a thousand executions; endorsement as evidence
  (Jordan, 1984 onward — the Air Jordan line's reported multi-billion annual revenue shows a persona can
  become a sub-brand); courage as positioning — taking cultural stances keeps the brand meaning active rather
  than nostalgic.
- **Visual principles:** The swoosh works in any single color at any size; photography style — grain, sweat,
  low angles — is as codified as the logo; typography (condensed, uppercase Futura lineage) delivers the
  imperative voice visually.
- **Learn:** Build a verbal asset with the same discipline as a visual one; a tagline maintained for decades
  becomes a second logo that works on the radio and in conversation.
- **Do NOT copy:** Logo-only confidence before earning it — dropping your name works after billions of
  impressions, not before. Borrowed athlete-worship without product credibility reads as sponsorship, not
  brand.
- **Suitable industries:** Apparel, fitness, tools, education, finance apps — any category where the customer
  buys a better version of themselves.

## LEGO (brick patented 1958; "System of Play" from 1955, Billund, Denmark)

- **Why world-class:** The 1958 brick still interlocks with bricks molded today — 65+ years of deliberate
  backward compatibility, held to reported molding tolerances in the hundredths of a millimeter. The product
  is the brand: the stud-and-tube coupling, the exact colors, the minifigure (1978) are all registered,
  recognizable assets. After near-bankruptcy in 2003 from diversifying away from the brick, LEGO refocused on
  the core system and became (reported) the world's largest toy company by revenue.
- **Design principles:** The system is sacred, the sets are disposable — every new theme must be compatible
  with every old brick; constraint breeds creativity (a small vocabulary of elements yields unbounded output);
  quality tolerance as brand promise — a brick that doesn't clutch is a broken promise.
- **Business problem solved:** Toys are hit-driven and fashion-mortal; a compatible system converts every
  purchase into an installment in a permanent collection, raising switching costs with each set and letting
  the brand ride licensed fads (Star Wars, 1999) without becoming one.
- **Psychology:** The endowment and IKEA effects — things you built yourself are valued above their parts;
  collection completion drives repeat purchase; intergenerational transfer — parents hand down bricks that
  still work, recruiting the next cohort at zero acquisition cost.
- **UX principles:** The clutch force of the brick is an engineered interaction — firm enough to hold, loose
  enough for a child's fingers; instructions communicate wordlessly across every language and age.
- **Branding principles:** Product-as-asset — the brick's shape and coupling are protected and promoted like a
  logo; the near-death of 2003 codified a rule: extensions must strengthen the system, not escape it; play
  values stated and enforced ("only the best is good enough," the founder's motto, functions as a quality
  constitution).
- **Visual principles:** A fixed, saturated primary color palette maintained for decades; the minifigure's
  proportions as an instantly attributable silhouette; red logo tile unchanged in essentials since the 1970s.
- **Learn:** Backward compatibility is a brand promise with compounding returns — every year of kept promise
  raises the cost of leaving and deepens trust; break it once and the collection logic collapses.
- **Do NOT copy:** The physical-system moat where no system exists — declaring an "ecosystem" without genuine
  interoperability is the software-era version of borrowed authority. Licensed-IP dependence works for LEGO
  because the brick outranks the license; a weaker system becomes a commodity vessel for someone else's brand.
- **Suitable industries:** Platforms and ecosystems of any kind — software APIs, modular furniture, camera
  systems, educational curricula — wherever compatibility promises can compound.

## Timeless principles extracted

- Distinctive beats different: invest in assets (color, shape, sound, script, tagline) that identify the brand
  instantly, and change campaigns freely while never touching the assets.
- A verbal asset maintained for decades is a second logo; write taglines as 30-year infrastructure, not
  campaign copy, and reject any line that only fits this year's product.
- Grow by being easy to think of and easy to buy for the whole category population; sharpening the "target
  audience" below the category's natural buyers shrinks the brand's memory footprint.
- Compatibility and consistency are promises with compounding interest — every kept year raises switching
  costs; a single break (reformulation, redesign, broken interoperability) spends decades of trust at once.
- When the product is copyable, move the decision to identity or system membership — but only after the
  product itself is beyond functional reproach; image built on a weak product amplifies the flaws.
- The customer defends the memory, not the object: before changing anything customers touch daily, assume you
  are renegotiating a ritual, and price the backlash into the decision.
